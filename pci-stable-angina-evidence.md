# Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (PCI) for Stable Angina
## An Evidence-Based Analysis of Benefit and Harm

---

## Executive Summary

Multiple large randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that routine PCI does not reduce death or myocardial infarction compared to optimal medical therapy (OMT) in patients with stable angina. While PCI may provide symptomatic benefit in selected patient populations, optimal medical therapy—particularly high-intensity statin therapy—has fundamentally changed the natural history of stable coronary disease. This analysis examines the evidence base for PCI in stable angina, with particular attention to patient selection, physiologic assessment, and the evolution of medical therapy.

---

## The Historical Context: The "Plaque-Busting" Doctrine

### The Foundation (1990s-2000s)

The entire field of interventional cardiology was built on a deceptively intuitive premise:

**The "Oculo-Stenotic Reflex"**: *See a blockage, fix a blockage*

**Core Assumptions:**
- Significant coronary stenosis (>70%) = "ticking time bomb" for heart attack
- Stenting = life-saving, prognostic intervention
- The degree of stenosis was a direct proxy for risk of myocardial infarction (MI)

### The Original Evidence Base

**RITA-2 (1997)** and similar early trials:
- Compared PCI to "medical therapy" for stable symptoms
- **Finding**: PCI superior for symptom control
- **Critical Context**: Medical therapy in the mid-1990s consisted primarily of symptomatic treatment (aspirin, beta-blocker, long-acting nitrates) without high-intensity statin therapy
- **Assumption**: Symptomatic benefit implied prognostic benefit

### The Resulting Doctrine

**Clinical Guidelines Built Around:**
1. Revascularization to prevent future cardiac events
2. Symptom relief as secondary benefit
3. Aggressive intervention for any significant stenosis

**Market Impact**: Multi-billion dollar industry, millions of procedures annually

## Procedural Risks and Complications

### Peri-procedural Risks

**Combined NNH**: **20-50** (2-5% risk of major complications)

**Major Complications Include:**
- Death
- Stroke
- Emergency coronary artery bypass surgery (CABG)
- Major bleeding
- Vascular complications
- Contrast-induced nephropathy

**Critical Point**: This harm is immediate, measurable, and unavoidable. Every patient accepts this risk up-front.

---

## The Evolution of Optimal Medical Therapy (OMT)

### The Paradigm Shift

The "medical therapy" of the 1990s was **symptomatic treatment only**:
- Aspirin
- Beta-blocker
- Long-acting nitrate

### The Discovery: High-Intensity Statins

**The Game-Changer**: High-dose statins were not just cholesterol-lowering drugs. They are the **most powerful plaque-stabilizing agents in existence**.

**Mechanism of Action:**
1. "Cools off" inflamed coronary plaques
2. Thickens the fibrous cap
3. Dramatically reduces rupture risk (the true cause of MI)

**The Carotid Parallel**: Just like in carotid artery disease, statins transformed the pathophysiology by targeting plaque *stability*, not stenosis *severity*.

### The Causal Model Failure

**The 1990s Model (WRONG)**:
```
Stenosis % → Flow Limitation → MI Risk
```

**The Actual Pathophysiology**:
```
Plaque Instability → Rupture → Thrombosis → MI
```

**Critical Insight**: Stenosis severity and plaque vulnerability are **poorly correlated**. A 50% stenosis with an inflamed, vulnerable cap is more dangerous than a stable 80% stenosis.

### Modern Optimal Medical Therapy (OMT)

**Components:**
1. High-intensity statin (atorvastatin 80mg or rosuvastatin 40mg)
2. ACE inhibitor or ARB
3. Aspirin (antiplatelet)
4. Beta-blocker (for angina control)
5. Lifestyle modification

**Result**: Transformed stable angina from a "pre-MI" state into a **benign, chronic inflammatory condition** with excellent prognosis.

**Baseline Risk Collapse**: The annual MI risk in the OMT control group plummeted from 5-8% (1990s) to <1% (modern era).

## Major Clinical Trials

### 1. COURAGE Trial (2007) – PCI Plus OMT vs. OMT Alone

**Design**: Multicenter randomized controlled trial
- **N**: 2,287 patients with stable angina and significant stenosis
- **Arms**:
  - PCI + OMT
  - OMT Alone
- **Follow-up**: 5 years

**Primary Endpoint**: Death or myocardial infarction

**Results**:
| Outcome | PCI + OMT | OMT Alone | p-value |
|---------|-----------|-----------|---------|
| Death or MI | 19.0% | 18.5% | 0.62 (NS) |

**Finding**: No statistically significant difference in death or MI over 5 years.

**Analysis**:
- PCI plus OMT did not demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in death or MI compared to OMT alone
- The low event rate in the OMT-alone group suggests that medical therapy effectively reduces cardiovascular risk in stable angina
- These results challenged the assumption that PCI provides prognostic benefit in stable coronary disease

**Citation**: Boden WE, et al. N Engl J Med. 2007;356(15):1503-1516.

---

### 2. ORBITA Trial (2018) – Sham-Controlled Trial of Symptomatic Benefit

**Design**: Double-blind, sham-controlled randomized controlled trial

**Background**: Following COURAGE, the focus shifted to symptomatic benefit as a primary indication for PCI. ORBITA was designed to rigorously test this hypothesis using a placebo control.

**Method**:
- **N**: 200 patients with stable angina and ≥70% stenosis
- **Arms**:
  - PCI + OMT
  - **Sham procedure** + OMT (catheter inserted, no stent placed)
- **Blinding**: Patients, physicians, and outcome assessors all blinded
- **Primary endpoint**: Exercise time on treadmill

**Results**:

| Outcome | PCI + OMT | Sham + OMT | Difference | p-value |
|---------|-----------|------------|------------|---------|
| Exercise time increase | +28.4 sec | +11.8 sec | +16.6 sec | 0.20 (NS) |
| Angina frequency | Improved | Improved | No difference | NS |

**Finding**: PCI did not demonstrate a statistically significant improvement in exercise time or angina frequency compared to sham procedure.

**Analysis**:
- The difference in exercise time improvement (+16.6 seconds) was not statistically significant (p=0.20)
- Both groups showed improvement from baseline, suggesting benefits from optimization of medical therapy and placebo effects
- This trial raised questions about the magnitude of symptomatic benefit attributable to PCI itself when patients are on background medical therapy

**Important Limitations**:
- Small sample size (N=200) may have limited power to detect modest differences
- Single-vessel disease only; not generalizable to multivessel CAD
- All patients were on optimized medical therapy
- Short follow-up period (6 weeks)

**Citation**: Al-Lamee R, et al. Lancet. 2018;391(10115):31-40.

---

### 3. ORBITA-2 Trial (2023) – Symptomatic Benefit Without Antianginal Medication

**Design**: Double-blind, sham-controlled randomized controlled trial

**Background**: ORBITA-2 was designed to test whether PCI provides symptomatic benefit in patients with angina who are NOT taking antianginal medications, addressing a key limitation of the original ORBITA trial.

**Method**:
- **N**: 301 patients with stable angina and ≥70% stenosis
- **Arms**:
  - PCI (n=151)
  - Sham procedure (n=150)
- **Key difference from ORBITA**: Patients discontinued antianginal medications at enrollment
- **Blinding**: Patients, physicians, and outcome assessors all blinded
- **Primary endpoint**: Angina symptom score at 12 weeks
- **Follow-up**: 12 weeks

**Results**:

| Outcome | PCI | Sham | Odds Ratio | p-value |
|---------|-----|------|------------|---------|
| Angina symptom score | 2.9 | 5.6 | 2.21 (1.41-3.47) | <0.001 |
| Daily angina episodes | 0.3 | 0.7 | - | <0.001 |
| Exercise time (seconds) | 700.9 | 641.4 | - | <0.001 |
| Freedom from angina | 3x higher | - | - | <0.001 |

**Finding**: PCI demonstrated statistically significant improvement in angina symptoms and exercise capacity compared to sham procedure.

**Analysis**:
- In contrast to ORBITA, ORBITA-2 showed clear symptomatic benefit of PCI over placebo
- The key difference appears to be the absence of background antianginal medication therapy
- Results suggest PCI provides symptomatic benefit in patients with ongoing angina who are not on (or cannot tolerate) antianginal medications
- This trial supports PCI as an option for symptom relief in selected patients with stable angina

**Important Context**:
- Patients in this trial were NOT on antianginal medications, which differs from typical clinical practice
- In real-world practice, most patients would receive medical therapy before considering PCI
- The trial demonstrates that PCI has a physiologic effect on angina symptoms when medications are not being used

**Citation**: Al-Lamee R, et al. N Engl J Med. 2024;390(2):115-125.

---

### 4. ISCHEMIA Trial (2020) – Invasive vs. Conservative Strategy

**Design**: Large-scale international trial
- **N**: 5,179 patients with moderate-to-severe ischemia
- **Arms**:
  - Initial invasive strategy (PCI or CABG) + OMT
  - Conservative strategy (OMT alone)
- **Follow-up**: Median 3.2 years

**Primary Endpoint**: Composite of cardiovascular death, MI, or hospitalization for unstable angina

**Results**:
| Outcome | Invasive + OMT | Conservative OMT | HR (95% CI) |
|---------|----------------|------------------|-------------|
| Primary endpoint | 13.3% | 15.5% | 0.93 (0.80-1.08) |
| Death or MI | 11.7% | 13.9% | 0.95 (0.80-1.12) |

**Finding**: No statistically significant difference in death, MI, or major cardiovascular events between invasive and conservative strategies.

**Quality of Life**: The invasive strategy showed a modest early advantage in angina-related symptoms, but this difference diminished over time, with minimal difference by 3 years.

**Analysis**:
- Confirmed COURAGE findings in a larger, more diverse population with documented moderate-to-severe ischemia
- OMT alone was non-inferior to an initial invasive strategy for hard cardiovascular outcomes
- These results do not support routine invasive management for prognostic benefit in stable coronary disease, even with significant ischemia

**Citation**: Maron DJ, et al. N Engl J Med. 2020;382(15):1395-1407.

---

### 5. FAME-2 Trial (2012, 5-Year Follow-up 2018) – FFR-Guided PCI

**Design**: Multicenter randomized controlled trial using fractional flow reserve (FFR) to identify hemodynamically significant stenoses

**Background**: Unlike the trials above that used angiographic criteria for stenosis severity, FAME-2 used physiologic assessment (FFR ≤0.80) to identify flow-limiting lesions. This represents a fundamentally different approach to patient selection.

**Method**:
- **N**: 888 patients with stable CAD and at least one stenosis with FFR ≤0.80
- **Arms**:
  - FFR-guided PCI + OMT (n=447)
  - OMT alone (n=441)
- **Additional registry**: 166 patients with FFR >0.80 received OMT only (demonstrating good outcomes without stenosis)
- **Primary endpoint**: Composite of death, MI, or urgent revascularization
- **Follow-up**: 5 years

**Results (5-year data)**:

| Outcome | PCI + OMT | OMT Alone | HR (95% CI) | p-value |
|---------|-----------|-----------|-------------|---------|
| Death, MI, or urgent revascularization | 13.9% | 27.0% | 0.46 (0.34-0.63) | <0.001 |
| Death | 5.1% | 5.2% | - | NS |
| MI | 8.1% | 12.0% | - | 0.11 (NS) |
| Urgent revascularization | 4.3% | 16.3% | - | <0.001 |

**Finding**: FFR-guided PCI significantly reduced the composite endpoint, driven primarily by a reduction in urgent revascularization.

**Analysis**:
- The benefit was largely driven by reduction in urgent revascularization, not hard endpoints (death/MI alone)
- There was a numerical trend toward reduced MI with PCI (8.1% vs 12.0%) but this did not reach statistical significance
- FFR-guided patient selection may identify a population more likely to benefit from PCI
- This contrasts with angiography-guided PCI (COURAGE, ISCHEMIA) where no benefit was seen

**Important Considerations**:
- The trial was stopped early for benefit, which may have overestimated treatment effect
- "Urgent revascularization" is a softer endpoint than death/MI and subject to bias
- Patients with FFR >0.80 (no hemodynamically significant stenosis) had excellent outcomes on OMT alone, supporting physiologic assessment
- These results suggest that *patient selection* based on physiology rather than anatomy may be important

**Interpretation**:
- FAME-2 suggests that FFR-guided PCI may have a role in stable CAD when hemodynamically significant stenosis is documented
- However, the lack of mortality benefit and modest effect on MI suggests the primary benefit is symptomatic/quality of life
- This trial highlights the importance of identifying which patients are most likely to benefit from intervention

**Citation**: Fearon WF, et al. N Engl J Med. 2018;379(3):250-259.

## Summary of Trial Evidence: The Importance of Patient Selection

### Key Findings by Trial and Patient Population

| Trial | Population | Intervention Type | Prognostic Benefit (Death/MI)? | Symptomatic Benefit? |
|-------|------------|-------------------|-------------------------------|---------------------|
| **COURAGE** | Stable angina, angiographic stenosis | Angiography-guided PCI + OMT | No significant benefit | Modest early benefit |
| **ORBITA** | Stable angina, single vessel, on OMT | Angiography-guided PCI + OMT | Not assessed | No significant benefit vs. placebo |
| **ORBITA-2** | Stable angina, OFF antianginal meds | Angiography-guided PCI | Not assessed | **Significant benefit vs. placebo** |
| **ISCHEMIA** | Moderate-severe ischemia | Invasive strategy (PCI/CABG) + OMT | No significant benefit | Modest early benefit, diminishes by 3y |
| **FAME-2** | FFR ≤0.80 (physiologic stenosis) | FFR-guided PCI + OMT | No mortality benefit; trend to ↓MI | **Significant ↓ urgent revasc** |

### Evidence-Based Conclusions

**For Prognostic Benefit (Preventing Death/MI)**:
- Angiography-guided PCI does not demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in death or MI compared to OMT alone (COURAGE, ISCHEMIA)
- FFR-guided PCI shows a numerical trend toward reduced MI but this did not reach statistical significance in FAME-2
- **Conclusion**: Routine PCI for stable angina is not supported for improving survival or preventing MI

**For Symptomatic Benefit**:
- Context-dependent and influenced by background medical therapy
- ORBITA-2 demonstrated clear symptomatic benefit when patients are NOT on antianginal medications
- ORBITA showed no significant benefit when patients are ON optimized medical therapy
- FAME-2 showed reduction in urgent revascularization (primarily a symptomatic endpoint)
- **Conclusion**: PCI may provide symptomatic benefit in selected patients, particularly those with ongoing symptoms despite (or off) medical therapy

**For Physiologic Assessment**:
- FFR-guided approach (FAME-2) showed different results than angiography-guided approach (COURAGE, ISCHEMIA)
- Patients with FFR >0.80 had excellent outcomes on OMT alone
- **Conclusion**: Physiologic assessment may help identify patients more likely to benefit from intervention

### Procedural Risk Assessment

**Modern PCI Complication Rates** (stable CAD, contemporary techniques):

Based on data from COURAGE, ISCHEMIA, and contemporary registries:

| Complication | Rate | Source |
|--------------|------|--------|
| Death | 0.2-0.5% | COURAGE, ISCHEMIA, ACC-NCDR |
| Stroke | 0.2-0.3% | Contemporary registries |
| Emergency CABG | 0.3-0.5% | COURAGE, ISCHEMIA |
| Major bleeding (BARC 3-5) | 1-2% | Contemporary series with radial access |
| Acute kidney injury | 1-3% | Varies with baseline renal function |
| **Major complications (composite)** | **~2-3%** | **NNH ≈ 33-50** |

**Important Considerations**:
- Complication rates have decreased with modern techniques (radial access, newer stents, bivalirudin)
- Risk varies significantly based on patient factors (age, renal function, bleeding risk) and lesion complexity
- CTO and complex multivessel PCI have higher complication rates than simple single-vessel PCI
- Procedural MI (using modern definitions) occurs in 3-5% but prognostic significance is debated

**Trade-offs**:
- ISCHEMIA showed early increase in procedural MI with invasive strategy, but trend toward fewer spontaneous MIs later
- The clinical significance of small periprocedural biomarker elevations vs. spontaneous MI is uncertain

**References**:
- Boden WE, et al. N Engl J Med. 2007;356(15):1503-1516 (COURAGE procedural data)
- Maron DJ, et al. N Engl J Med. 2020;382(15):1395-1407 (ISCHEMIA procedural data)
- Rao SV, et al. JACC Cardiovasc Interv. 2016;9(12):1227-1235 (Radial vs femoral access meta-analysis)

## Historical Context: Evolution of Medical Therapy and Evidence

### Historical Control Bias in Early Trials

**Problem**: Meta-analyses including pre-2000 trials are comparing:
- **PCI + OMT** (modern)
- vs. **Symptomatic therapy alone** (aspirin + beta-blocker)

**This is NOT a comparison of PCI vs. no-PCI**. It's a comparison of **OMT vs. no-OMT**, with PCI as a confounding variable.

### The Causal Severance

**The 1990s Causal Chain**:
```
Stenosis → Ischemia → Plaque disruption → MI
```
**Therefore**: Remove stenosis → Prevent MI ✗ (WRONG)

**The Actual Causal Chain**:
```
Inflammation → Vulnerable plaque → Rupture → Thrombosis → MI
```
**Key Insight**: Stenosis severity ≠ Plaque vulnerability

**What Statins Do**: Attack the *actual* causal mechanism
```
Statins → ↓ Inflammation → Stable plaque → No rupture → No MI ✓
```

**Result**: Statins severed the link between stenosis and outcomes. The old model became obsolete.

---

## Methodological Lessons

### 1. The Surrogate Endpoint Trap

**Stenosis %** was treated as a validated surrogate for MI risk. It was not.

**Lesson**: Surrogate endpoints must be validated against hard outcomes, not assumed.

### 2. The Importance of Sham Controls

**Before ORBITA**: 40 years of "obvious" symptomatic benefit
**After ORBITA**: 100% of the benefit was placebo + OMT

**Lesson**: Subjective endpoints (chest pain) *require* sham controls to separate procedure from placebo.

### 3. The "Mechanism Matters" Principle

**The Error**: Assumed the mechanism of MI was flow limitation from stenosis
**The Reality**: Mechanism is plaque rupture, largely independent of stenosis severity

**Lesson**: Treating the wrong mechanism, even effectively, produces no benefit.

### 4. The Drug-Induced Evidence Decay

**Novel Insight**: OMT didn't just *add* benefit. It *eliminated* the disease mechanism that PCI targeted.

**Result**: Old evidence became not just dated, but **fundamentally inapplicable**.

## Limitations and Areas of Uncertainty

### Trial Limitations

**COURAGE**:
- Excluded high-risk anatomic features (left main, severe proximal LAD)
- Enrollment period predated some contemporary stent technologies
- Medical therapy adherence varied over long-term follow-up

**ORBITA**:
- Small sample size (N=200) may have limited power to detect modest symptomatic benefits
- Single-vessel disease only; not representative of many PCI candidates
- Short follow-up (6 weeks) does not assess long-term symptomatic benefit
- All patients on optimized medical therapy

**ORBITA-2**:
- Patients were OFF antianginal medications, which differs from typical clinical practice where medical therapy would be optimized first
- Relatively short follow-up (12 weeks)
- Does not address the question of PCI benefit when medical therapy is maximized

**ISCHEMIA**:
- Excluded left main disease and recent ACS
- Did not specifically examine FFR-guided PCI
- Some crossover between groups (21% of conservative group underwent revascularization)
- Question about equipoise given known benefit in high-risk subsets

**FAME-2**:
- Stopped early for benefit, potentially overestimating treatment effect
- Primary endpoint driven by urgent revascularization (softer endpoint than death/MI)
- "Urgent revascularization" subject to ascertainment bias
- No sham control for symptomatic assessment

### Remaining Clinical Questions

1. **Optimal timing of PCI**: When medical therapy is partially effective, at what threshold of residual symptoms should PCI be considered?

2. **Long-term symptomatic benefit**: ORBITA-2 showed benefit at 12 weeks off medications, but what about long-term follow-up?

3. **FFR threshold**: Is 0.80 the optimal FFR cutoff, or should it be individualized?

4. **Complete vs. culprit-only revascularization**: Limited data in stable multivessel disease

5. **Subgroup heterogeneity**: Are there clinical or anatomic features that predict greater benefit from PCI?

6. **Quality of life**: More granular assessment of QOL benefits beyond angina frequency

7. **Cost-effectiveness**: In the modern era with lower complication rates and clearer patient selection

### Populations Not Well-Studied

- Left main disease (excluded from COURAGE, ISCHEMIA)
- Very elderly patients (>80 years)
- Severe chronic kidney disease
- Patients with prior CABG
- CTO in the context of multivessel disease
- Angina with non-obstructive CAD (microvascular dysfunction)

### Technological Evolution

- Trials used technologies available at enrollment (2000s-2010s)
- Modern developments not captured in landmark trials:
  - Newer-generation drug-eluting stents
  - Intravascular imaging (IVUS, OCT) guidance
  - Coronary physiology beyond FFR (iFR, diastolic pressure ratios)
  - Radial access (lower bleeding/vascular complications)

## Current Guidelines (Post-ISCHEMIA)

### Major Society Recommendations

**American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (2021)**:
- **Class I**: OMT for all patients with stable ischemic heart disease
- **Class IIa**: PCI may be reasonable for:
  - Refractory angina despite guideline-directed medical therapy
  - Patients who are unable to tolerate antianginal medications
  - Left main disease or multivessel disease with reduced LVEF
- **Class III (No Benefit)**: Routine PCI to improve survival in stable CAD without anatomic high-risk features

**European Society of Cardiology (2019, updated 2020)**:
- OMT as first-line therapy for all patients with chronic coronary syndromes
- Revascularization (PCI or CABG) for:
  - Prognostic indications: left main, multivessel disease with reduced LVEF
  - Symptomatic indications: angina limiting quality of life despite medical therapy
- Explicit statement: Revascularization does not reduce mortality in most patients with stable CAD
- Consider FFR/iFR to guide revascularization decisions

### Evolution of Guideline Recommendations

**Historical (Pre-COURAGE, ~2000s)**:
- PCI recommended to prevent MI and improve symptoms in patients with significant stenosis
- Emphasis on anatomic severity (>70% stenosis)
- Medical therapy viewed as adjunct to revascularization

**Modern (Post-COURAGE, ISCHEMIA, ORBITA-2, ~2020s)**:
- OMT as foundation of treatment for all patients
- PCI reserved for specific indications:
  - Anatomic high-risk features (prognostic)
  - Refractory symptoms (symptomatic/QOL)
- Emphasis on physiologic assessment (FFR/iFR) over anatomy alone
- Shared decision-making incorporating patient preferences, symptom burden, and treatment goals

### Current Consensus

**Areas of Agreement**:
- OMT should be optimized in all patients before considering PCI for symptom relief
- PCI does not reduce mortality in most patients with stable angina
- High-risk anatomic features (left main, proximal LAD, multivessel with reduced LVEF) may warrant revascularization
- Physiologic assessment helps identify hemodynamically significant stenosis

**Areas of Ongoing Discussion**:
- Optimal threshold for "refractory" symptoms warranting PCI
- Role of complete revascularization in multivessel disease
- Integration of ORBITA-2 results: should patients trial off medications to assess PCI benefit?
- Use of advanced imaging (IVUS, OCT) to guide intervention

## Understanding the "Common Sense" Intuition

### Why This Persists

**Intuitive Appeal**: "If there's a blockage, shouldn't we unblock it?"

**Visual Bias**: Angiograms showing severe stenosis are visually compelling

**Immediate Gratification**: Patients feel better immediately (placebo + procedure ritual)

**Financial Incentives**: Massive industry with entrenched interests

**Cognitive Dissonance**: Difficult for interventional cardiologists to accept that their life's work may not provide benefit

### The Counter-Intuitive Truth

**Reality**: The heart is resilient.
- Collateral circulation develops around chronic blockages
- Stable plaques are not "about to cause a heart attack"
- The degree of stenosis visible on angiography is a poor predictor of future events
- The *real* danger is invisible: thin-capped, inflamed plaques that may cause only 30-40% stenosis

**The Statin Miracle**: Stabilizes the vulnerable plaque, regardless of stenosis degree

## Clinical Implications and Future Directions

### What Should Replace PCI?

**For Stable Angina:**
1. **Aggressive OMT**: High-dose statin, antiplatelet, ACE-I, beta-blocker
2. **Lifestyle modification**: Exercise, diet, smoking cessation
3. **Symptom management**: Titrate anti-anginal medications
4. **Watchful waiting**: Most patients improve on OMT alone

**Reserved for**:
- **Acute coronary syndromes** (STEMI, NSTEMI) - where PCI *is* life-saving
- **True refractory angina** limiting quality of life despite maximal OMT
- **Left main or severe three-vessel disease** with poor LV function

### Ongoing Research

**Emerging Questions**:
1. Can advanced imaging (FFR-CT, OCT) identify the subset who truly benefit?
2. What is the real placebo-adjusted NNT for symptom relief in OMT-refractory patients?
3. How do we de-implement a practice embedded in practice patterns and economics?

## Summary and Clinical Recommendations

### Evidence-Based Conclusions

**For Routine PCI in Stable Angina**:

Multiple large randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that:

1. **Prognostic Benefit**: Routine PCI does not reduce death or myocardial infarction compared to optimal medical therapy alone
   - COURAGE, ISCHEMIA, and COURAGE extended follow-up all showed no mortality benefit
   - This conclusion applies to both angiography-guided and ischemia-guided strategies

2. **Symptomatic Benefit**: The evidence is nuanced and depends on patient selection and medical therapy:
   - **When patients are ON antianginal medications**: ORBITA showed no significant benefit over placebo
   - **When patients are OFF antianginal medications**: ORBITA-2 showed clear symptomatic benefit
   - **Clinical implication**: PCI may provide symptomatic relief in patients with ongoing angina despite (or intolerant of) medical therapy

3. **The Role of Physiologic Assessment**:
   - FFR-guided PCI (FAME-2) showed reduction in urgent revascularization, driven primarily by symptom relief
   - Patients with FFR >0.80 had excellent outcomes on medical therapy alone
   - Physiologic assessment may help identify patients more likely to benefit from intervention

### Clinical Approach to Stable Angina

**First-Line Therapy** (all patients):
- **Optimal medical therapy** including:
  - High-intensity statin (atorvastatin 80mg or rosuvastatin 40mg)
  - Antiplatelet therapy (aspirin)
  - ACE inhibitor or ARB
  - Beta-blocker for angina control
  - Lifestyle modification (exercise, diet, smoking cessation)
- OMT alone provides excellent prognostic outcomes in stable CAD

**Consider PCI** in selected patients:
- **Refractory angina** limiting quality of life despite maximal medical therapy
- Patients with FFR-documented hemodynamically significant stenosis and ongoing symptoms
- Patients intolerant of or unable to take antianginal medications
- **Anatomic high-risk features**: left main disease, severe proximal LAD stenosis, multivessel disease with reduced LV function

**Do NOT routinely use PCI for**:
- Prognostic benefit (preventing death/MI) in stable angina
- Asymptomatic patients with angiographic stenosis
- Patients with well-controlled symptoms on medical therapy

### Key Insights from the Evidence

**Patient Selection Matters**:
- Not all patients with coronary stenosis benefit equally from PCI
- Physiologic assessment (FFR/iFR) identifies hemodynamically significant lesions
- Background medical therapy influences symptomatic benefit

**Optimal Medical Therapy Works**:
- High-intensity statins fundamentally changed the natural history of stable CAD
- OMT reduces MI risk by stabilizing vulnerable plaques, not by reducing stenosis severity
- The low event rates in modern medical therapy arms (COURAGE, ISCHEMIA) demonstrate the effectiveness of OMT

**Mechanistic Understanding**:
- Stenosis severity is an imperfect predictor of MI risk
- Plaque vulnerability and inflammation are key drivers of acute coronary events
- Statins address plaque stability; PCI addresses stenosis—these target different mechanisms

**Methodological Rigor**:
- Sham-controlled trials (ORBITA, ORBITA-2) were essential to separate placebo effects from true procedural benefit
- The difference in ORBITA vs ORBITA-2 results highlights the importance of background therapy
- Surrogate endpoints (stenosis reduction) do not reliably predict clinical benefit

### The Balanced Perspective

PCI for stable angina represents an important case study in evidence-based medicine:
- Initial enthusiasm based on intuitive mechanistic reasoning
- Refined understanding through rigorous randomized trials
- Recognition that patient selection and context determine benefit
- Evolution of guidelines to reflect evidence

**The evidence does NOT support**:
- Routine PCI for prognostic benefit in stable angina
- PCI in asymptomatic patients based solely on angiographic stenosis

**The evidence DOES support**:
- OMT as first-line therapy for all patients with stable angina
- Selective use of PCI for symptom relief in appropriate patients
- Physiologic assessment to guide intervention decisions
- Individualized treatment based on symptoms, anatomy, physiology, and response to medical therapy

**The Clinical Message**: In stable angina, start with optimal medical therapy. Reserve PCI for patients with ongoing limiting symptoms despite medical management, or those with high-risk anatomic features. Patient selection and shared decision-making are essential.
