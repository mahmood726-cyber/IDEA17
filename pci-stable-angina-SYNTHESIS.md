# Percutaneous Coronary Intervention for Stable Angina: An Evidence-Based Synthesis

## Abstract

Multiple large randomized controlled trials have fundamentally challenged the role of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in stable angina. This synthesis examines key evidence demonstrating that routine PCI does not reduce death or myocardial infarction compared to optimal medical therapy (OMT), while symptomatic benefit depends critically on patient selection and medication status. The evolution of high-intensity statin therapy has transformed stable coronary disease from a progressive condition requiring mechanical intervention to a manageable chronic inflammatory state responsive to pharmacotherapy.

---

## Introduction

For decades, interventional cardiology operated on an intuitive premise: significant coronary stenosis represents a "ticking time bomb" requiring mechanical relief to prevent myocardial infarction and death. This "oculo-stenotic reflex"—see a blockage, fix a blockage—drove a multi-billion dollar industry performing millions of procedures annually. However, landmark trials over the past two decades have systematically dismantled this paradigm, revealing that the fundamental assumptions were flawed and that optimal medical therapy, particularly high-intensity statins, has rendered much of the traditional rationale for PCI obsolete.

## The Evidence Base: Five Landmark Trials

### COURAGE (2007): No Prognostic Benefit

The COURAGE trial randomized 2,287 patients with stable angina and significant stenosis to PCI plus OMT versus OMT alone. After 4.6 years of follow-up, the primary endpoint of death or myocardial infarction occurred in 19.0% of the PCI group versus 18.5% of the OMT-alone group (p=0.62)—no statistically significant difference. Extended 15-year follow-up confirmed these findings: PCI plus OMT conferred no mortality benefit compared to medical therapy alone. The trial challenged the fundamental assumption that relieving stenosis prevents cardiovascular events in stable disease.

### ORBITA (2018): Questioning Symptomatic Benefit

When prognostic justifications failed, proponents retreated to symptomatic relief as the primary indication. ORBITA rigorously tested this with a double-blind, sham-controlled design—200 patients with stable angina and ≥70% stenosis received either PCI or a sham procedure, both with background OMT. The difference in exercise time improvement was not statistically significant (+16.6 seconds, p=0.20). Both groups improved from baseline, suggesting benefits arose from medication optimization and placebo effects rather than the stent itself.

### ORBITA-2 (2023): Context-Dependent Symptomatic Benefit

ORBITA-2 addressed a critical limitation: what if patients were not on antianginal medications? In 301 patients who discontinued antianginal drugs, PCI demonstrated clear symptomatic benefit compared to sham procedure. Angina symptom scores were 2.9 versus 5.6 (OR 2.21, 95% CI 1.41-3.47, p<0.001), with patients three times more likely to achieve freedom from angina with PCI. This trial revealed that symptomatic benefit depends critically on medication status: PCI provides relief when antianginal medications are absent or poorly tolerated, but not when patients are on optimized therapy.

### ISCHEMIA (2020): Confirming Findings in Ischemia-Driven Strategy

ISCHEMIA enrolled 5,179 patients with moderate-to-severe ischemia, testing whether even physiologically significant disease warranted intervention. The primary endpoint occurred in 13.3% of the invasive group versus 15.5% of the conservative group (HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.80-1.08)—no significant difference. The invasive strategy showed modest early symptomatic advantages that diminished by three years. These results confirmed that even documented ischemia does not mandate revascularization for prognostic benefit.

### FAME-2 (2018): The Physiology Question

FAME-2 took a different approach: fractional flow reserve (FFR) to identify hemodynamically significant stenoses. Among 888 patients with FFR ≤0.80, the composite endpoint of death, MI, or urgent revascularization occurred in 13.9% of the PCI group versus 27.0% of OMT alone (HR 0.46, p<0.001). However, this benefit was driven primarily by reduced urgent revascularization (4.3% vs 16.3%), not hard endpoints—mortality was identical (5.1% vs 5.2%), and MI reduction did not reach significance (8.1% vs 12.0%, p=0.11). Importantly, patients with FFR >0.80 had excellent outcomes on OMT alone, demonstrating that physiologic assessment identifies those unlikely to benefit from intervention.

## The Paradigm Shift: How Statins Changed Everything

The medical therapy of the 1990s was primitive—aspirin, a beta-blocker, perhaps a nitrate. The revolution came with recognition that high-intensity statins are not merely cholesterol-lowering agents but the most powerful plaque-stabilizing drugs in existence. Statins reduce inflammation, thicken the fibrous cap of vulnerable plaques, and dramatically reduce rupture risk—the actual mechanism of most myocardial infarctions.

The old model assumed stenosis severity predicted MI risk. The reality is more nuanced: plaque vulnerability and inflammation drive acute events, often at sites with only moderate stenosis. Annual MI rates in OMT control arms plummeted from 5-8% in the 1990s to <1% in modern trials. Statins severed the link between stenosis and outcomes by targeting the true causal mechanism. Old evidence comparing PCI to primitive medical therapy became fundamentally inapplicable.

## Procedural Risk Considerations

Modern PCI is safer than historical procedures, but risk remains measurable. Contemporary data show death (0.2-0.5%), stroke (0.2-0.3%), emergency CABG (0.3-0.5%), and major bleeding (1-2%) for stable CAD procedures. The composite major complication rate of approximately 2-3% translates to a number needed to harm (NNH) of 33-50. While manageable, these risks must be weighed against potential benefits—a calculus that becomes unfavorable when prognostic benefit is absent and symptomatic benefit is context-dependent.

## Clinical Implications and Recommendations

The evidence supports a fundamentally different approach to stable angina:

**First-line therapy for all patients**: Optimal medical therapy including high-intensity statin (atorvastatin 80mg or rosuvastatin 40mg), antiplatelet therapy, ACE inhibitor or ARB, beta-blocker for symptom control, and lifestyle modification. OMT alone provides excellent prognostic outcomes.

**Consider PCI in selected patients**: (1) Refractory angina limiting quality of life despite maximal medical therapy; (2) FFR-documented hemodynamically significant stenosis with ongoing symptoms; (3) Intolerance to antianginal medications; (4) High-risk anatomic features including left main disease, severe proximal LAD stenosis, or multivessel disease with reduced left ventricular function.

**Do not routinely use PCI for**: (1) Prognostic benefit in stable angina without high-risk features; (2) Asymptomatic patients with angiographic stenosis; (3) Patients with well-controlled symptoms on medical therapy.

Current ACC/AHA and ESC guidelines reflect this evolution, designating OMT as Class I for all patients while limiting PCI to Class IIa for refractory symptoms or high-risk anatomy. Routine PCI for prognostic benefit receives a Class III (no benefit) recommendation.

## The Path Forward

Patient selection determines benefit. Not all stenoses require intervention; physiologic assessment with FFR or iFR helps identify hemodynamically significant lesions. Background medical therapy influences whether PCI provides symptomatic relief. The ORBITA/ORBITA-2 contrast demonstrates this elegantly: no benefit when medications are optimized, clear benefit when they are not.

Shared decision-making is essential. Patients should understand that PCI will not prevent heart attacks or prolong life in stable angina, but may relieve symptoms in specific circumstances. The intuitive appeal of "fixing a blockage" must be balanced against evidence showing that the blockage is often not the primary problem—the inflamed, vulnerable plaque is, and statins address this more effectively than stents.

## Conclusion

The evidence is clear: routine PCI for stable angina is not supported for prognostic benefit. Symptomatic benefit exists but is context-dependent, influenced by medication status and patient selection. Optimal medical therapy, anchored by high-intensity statins, should be the foundation of treatment. PCI retains a role for carefully selected patients with refractory symptoms or high-risk anatomy, but the era of reflexive intervention for angiographic stenosis has ended. This represents evidence-based medicine at its best—rigorous trials overturning intuitive assumptions to reveal more effective, less invasive treatment strategies.
